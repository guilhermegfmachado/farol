Formative Assessment

Assess to learn, not just to grade

What it is

  • Formative assessment is any practice that gathers information about learning during the process — and uses that information to adjust teaching or the support given to the student.1
  • Unlike summative assessment (final grades), formative assessment does not have to carry a mark — it has to carry feedback.4
  • For neurodivergent students, formative assessment matters especially because performance on a one-off test may not reflect what they actually know.1

Quick techniques

  • Exit ticket: at the end of the lesson, the student answers one question in 2 minutes — the teacher sees immediately who understood and who did not.3
  • Traffic light: green (I understood), amber (I have doubts), red (I did not understand) — physical cards, post-its, or digital.
  • Think-Pair-Share: the student thinks individually, discusses with a partner, then shares with the class — reducing the anxiety of answering directly.
  • Exit door: before leaving, the student tells the teacher one thing they learned and one question they still have.

Effective feedback

  • Effective feedback is specific, actionable, and timely: "on page 2, the argument is lost because the link to the evidence is missing" is useful; "could be better" is not.2,4
  • For students with ADHD: immediate feedback is more effective than delayed. Do not wait until the end of the task.2
  • For students with dyslexia: oral or audio feedback can be more accessible than extensive written comments.
  • Avoid comparing students with one another — compare the student with their own earlier progress.2

Adaptations by profile

  • ADHD: assessment across several short moments rather than one long test. Reduce distracting stimuli in the assessment setting.
  • Dyslexia: allow oral answers, extra time, or text-to-speech. Assess the content, not the spelling.
  • Autism: assessments with clear, unambiguous written instructions. Warn in advance about changes to the format.
  • Giftedness: avoid assessments that merely reproduce content — include questions of analysis, synthesis, and creation.

When to refer

  • When, even with assessment adaptations, the student cannot demonstrate learning, it is a sign that there may be a deeper barrier to investigate with the school guidance service.
  • Persistent patterns (months, not days) of specific difficulty in one area should be formally flagged.
