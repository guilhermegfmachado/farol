Profile 05

Giftedness

Significantly above-average ability, frequently under-stimulated

How it shows up in class

  • Finishes tasks in minutes while the class takes the whole lesson.
  • Asks questions beyond the scope of the material — or actively challenges what is being taught.
  • May appear disengaged, disruptive, or apathetic — often signs of chronic boredom and underachievement.2
  • Heightened emotional sensitivity; may be anxious or perfectionistic — psychosocial factors are decisive in the development of talent.1
  • Real risk of underachievement — may learn to hide their ability so as not to stand out socially.2

What it isn't

  • It is not a synonym for good behaviour or good grades — many gifted students perform around the average out of disinterest.2
  • It is not "does not need help": they need different challenges, because ability only becomes achievement when it is deliberately developed.1
  • It is not incompatible with neurodivergence — twice-exceptionality (2e) exists, is common, and is often masked.3

Strategies without AI

  • Always have extension tasks ready — not more of the same, but deeper or applied material.1
  • Allow independent investigation of topics that interest the student within the curriculum.
  • Assess by challenge, not by ease — ask for more demanding work in exchange for less volume.
  • Acknowledge and validate emotional sensitivity without pathologising it.

Talking to parents

  • Anticipate the pride and redirect it: "they are clearly very able — and that is precisely why the boredom is causing problems".
  • Explain the paradox of giftedness: good grades do not mean all is well, and underachievement through disinterest is common and reversible.2
  • If the student hides their ability to fit in socially, name it carefully — it is a sign of distress, not modesty.
  • Suggest the parent/guardian value process over result at home: curiosity, depth, and hard questions matter more than high marks on easy tasks.

Signs to refer for assessment

  • Signs of anxiety or depression associated with extreme perfectionism.1
  • Marked social isolation from feeling "different" from peers.
  • Sudden academic underachievement in a student previously identified as able.2

Informational, not clinical — when in doubt, consult the school psychology services or qualified professionals.

Overlapping profiles

  • Giftedness does not rule out needs: twice-exceptionality (with autism, ADHD or dyslexia) is precisely the coexistence of the two. A student can compensate for years and "look like" neither — average marks with exceptional reasoning are a signal, not a contradiction.
